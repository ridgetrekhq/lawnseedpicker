How to Overseed Clover Into an Existing Lawn

By Jon P. — founder of LawnSeedPicker and home-yard DIY enthusiast · Published August 22, 2026

You can add clover to a lawn you already have without killing the grass first — overseeding is the normal route. The technique differs from overseeding grass in two ways that matter: the seed is far smaller, so the rate is far lower, and there is a spring-only method worth knowing about.

Can you add clover to a lawn you already have?

Yes. Clover overseeds into existing turf rather than requiring a reset — no-till seeding into existing grass is a standard establishment method. For whether a clover lawn is the right call for your yard in the first place, see clover lawns.

When should you overseed clover?

Late summer to early fall is the primary window — the same timing logic as overseeding grass. See overseeding: fall vs. spring and when to plant grass seed for the reasoning behind that window. Spring is the backup. Clover establishes best in cool, moist weather on well-drained, fertile soil with a pH between 6 and 7. There's a third option, too: seeding while the ground is still frozen.

Frost seeding — the early-spring method

White clover can be frost seeded into existing grass in early spring, while the soil is still honeycombed with frost. No tilling or raking is required. White clover can also be no-till seeded into existing grass without waiting for frost.

The failure mode here is specific and worth stating plainly: you have to seed while the ground is still frozen. Delaying until midmorning, once the soil surface thaws and turns slippery, results in poor stand establishment. This method only works in regions that get hard freezes in late winter and early spring. If your ground doesn't freeze solid, skip it and stick to the late-summer or spring windows above.

How much clover seed do you need?

About 0.1 lb — 50 grams — per 1,000 sq ft, based on one seed vendor's overseeding rate for an established lawn. That's the number to start from, but it's worth knowing how shaky the ground under it is: vendor figures for this exact job disagree by roughly five to nine times, and no extension or USDA source publishes a clover lawn seeding rate in pounds per 1,000 square feet at all. Clover seed is tiny — roughly 500,000 seeds per pound — which is why the rate looks so low next to a grass seeding rate. How to overseed clover, step by step

Mow the lawn low and bag the clippings so they don't smother the new seed. Open the surface — rake vigorously or core aerate — to get seed-to-soil contact on a firm seedbed; a firm seedbed is what holds proper seeding depth. Broadcast the seed by hand or spreader; because the volume is so small, mixing it with sand or another carrier helps distribute it evenly instead of clumping in a few spots. Don't bury it — plant no deeper than ¼ inch. Keep the seedbed consistently moist through establishment, and reserve some seed to reapply for patching once the lawn is established.

What to expect after seeding

Clover spreads by stolons — creeping stems along the surface — and knits itself into the lawn gradually rather than filling in overnight. Once established, it tolerates foot traffic and helps reduce soil compaction. Give it a full season before judging the stand — patience is most of the job here. Frequently asked questions

Can you just throw clover seed on an existing lawn?

Not quite as-is — the seed needs soil contact to establish. Rake or core aerate the surface first, broadcast the seed, and keep it moist; mixing it with sand or another carrier helps spread such a small volume evenly. The one exception is frost seeding in early spring, which needs no soil prep.

What is frost seeding, and does it actually work?

Frost seeding means broadcasting clover seed in early spring while the ground is still frozen and honeycombed with frost, with no tilling or raking. It works, but only if timed right — you have to seed while the ground is still frozen, since delaying until the surface thaws and turns slippery in midmorning results in poor stand establishment. It's a technique for regions that get hard freezes.

How much clover seed do I need per 1,000 square feet?

About 0.1 lb, or 50 grams, per 1,000 square feet, based on one seed vendor's overseeding rate. Vendor figures for this exact job disagree by roughly five to nine times, and no extension source publishes a clover lawn rate at all, so treat this as a starting point, not a verified figure. Clover seed is tiny — roughly 500,000 seeds per pound — which is why the number looks so small next to a grass seeding rate.

Will clover take over my grass?

Not at typical overseeding rates. Clover spreads by stolons and knits in gradually rather than aggressively displacing grass, and vendor guidance for building a lawn from scratch keeps clover to around 5 percent of the total seed mix by weight — it's designed to be a minority share, not a takeover. How much you seed gives you control over how much clover ends up in the mix.

Do I need to kill my existing lawn before adding clover?

No. Clover overseeds directly into existing turf — no-till seeding into existing grass is a standard establishment method, and frost seeding in early spring works the same way. There's no need to strip or kill the lawn first; the grass and clover establish side by side.
