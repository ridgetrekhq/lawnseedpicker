Creeping Thyme Lawn: What the Research Says

By Jon P. — founder of LawnSeedPicker and home-yard DIY enthusiast · Published November 22, 2026

Creeping thyme works as a lawn substitute in small areas, not as a whole lawn. Illinois Extension describes it as suitable "as a lawn substitute in small areas and for planting between stepping stones." And the University of Minnesota, whose research helped put creeping thyme into bee lawns, now says it "is no longer recommended for bee lawn mixtures."

Can you use creeping thyme instead of grass?

In small areas, yes. Illinois Extension calls creeping thyme (Thymus serpyllum) a low-growing creeper suitable as a lawn substitute in small areas and between stepping stones. It prefers full sun to very light shade, needs well-drained soil, and is hardy in Zone 4. A University of Nevada, Reno fact sheet on lawn alternatives pictures it the same way: as a groundcover around a stepping-stone path. None of the sources cited on this page describes creeping thyme replacing a whole lawn.

Why the University of Minnesota stopped recommending it for bee lawns

University of Minnesota research found that white clover, self-heal and creeping thyme "work the best in a bee lawn." Its current Extension guide then adds: "due to recent long-term observations and the lack of seed availability, creeping thyme is no longer recommended for bee lawn mixtures."

A University of Minnesota research poster gives more detail. The bee lawn seed mix first sold by a Twin Cities seed vendor in 2018 included fine fescues, Dutch white clover, self-heal and creeping thyme. Under the heading "Reconsidering creeping thyme," the poster says consumers have a hard time establishing it "due to its small seed and it aggressively spreads once mature," and that Minnesota's Lawns to Legumes program is considering removing it as an option because of participants' use of cultivars and "potential invasiveness." A photo on the poster shows creeping thyme "dominating long-established bee lawn plots." That is a poster, not a peer-reviewed paper, and "considering" is not a decision.

The peer-reviewed study behind the bee lawn work tested eight flowering plants seeded into mowed hard fescue in central Minnesota. Creeping thyme was one of the four that established in at least one location.

Will creeping thyme take foot traffic?

The sources disagree, and the answer depends on which thyme you have. NC State Extension's plant database says Thymus praecox forms a dense mat that "tolerates moderate foot traffic" and "may be used as a lawn substitute or to fill in between pavers." For the cultivar 'Elfin', the same database says: "It will tolerate light foot traffic." A University of Arkansas horticulturist, writing about Thymus praecox, put it the other way: in walkways "the soil should be recessed because thymes do not tolerate foot traffic." None of these sources tested thyme as a lawn that gets walked on every day.

Elfin thyme and the naming problem

"Creeping thyme" is not one plant. The University of Arkansas column found "half a dozen species bearing this common name," and notes that thymes are often misidentified in garden centers and nurseries. Check the botanical name on the seed packet or plant tag.

'Elfin' is a cultivar of Thymus serpyllum. NC State says it spreads to 1 to 1.5 feet, prefers full sun in sandy loam or rocky soils with good drainage, and suits stepping stones, edging and rock gardens. It can suffer root rot on poorly drained soils.

How long does a creeping thyme planting last?

One extension horticulturist's observation: in well-drained rock gardens, creeping thyme spreads quickly the first couple of years, but "after three or four years the original part of the plant will become thin and begin dying." At that point, he wrote, the plant should be divided in late winter and the vigorous divisions used to reestablish it. That is one writer's experience in a column — the University of Arkansas notes it does not recommend the plants featured in the series — not a tested lifespan.

Seed or plugs?

The University of Minnesota calls creeping thyme "trickier to get established from seed" and notes that small seedlings, called plugs, are sometimes used instead. Plugs "can be hard to find," cost more, and "might only be an option for small sites." The Minnesota Ask Extension answer below adds that it takes time to fill in.

We found no extension seeding rate for a creeping thyme lawn. The University of Minnesota's current bee lawn seeding rates cover fine fescue, Dutch white clover and self-heal, and creeping thyme is not on the list. If you want to try seed on a small area, Outsidepride creeping thyme seed (Thymus serpyllum) names its species on the listing. Expect slow establishment.

Does creeping thyme stay green in winter?

In Minnesota, an Ask Extension answer says creeping thyme is "even green through the winter under the snow." The same answer says an edging barrier will keep it contained only if there is no soil for it to root in.

Is a thyme lawn OK with kids, dogs and bees?

Thyme draws bees. A University of Nevada, Reno fact sheet for southern Nevada describes red mother-of-thyme (Thymus serpyllum 'Coccineum') as "fragrant and bee-friendly, so it may not be well-suited for areas where pets or children will play." NC State lists creeping thyme as attracting bees and butterflies, and as deer and rabbit resistant.

A better bet for most yards: clover

For a lawn you walk on, the bee lawn the University of Minnesota still recommends keeps turfgrass as the base and adds flowers such as Dutch white clover and self-heal. Frequently asked questions

Can creeping thyme replace a lawn?

In small areas. Illinois Extension calls it suitable as a lawn substitute in small areas and between stepping stones, and none of the sources cited here describes it replacing a whole lawn.

Can you walk on creeping thyme?

Lightly, depending on the plant. NC State says Thymus praecox tolerates moderate foot traffic and 'Elfin' light foot traffic, while a University of Arkansas horticulturist wrote that thymes do not tolerate foot traffic in walkways.

Is creeping thyme invasive?

No source cited here calls it invasive outright. A University of Minnesota poster says it "aggressively spreads once mature" and that a Minnesota program is considering dropping it over "potential invasiveness."

Is creeping thyme better than clover for a lawn?

For a lawn people use, the University of Minnesota's answer points to clover: creeping thyme "is no longer recommended for bee lawn mixtures," while Dutch white clover stays in its recommended mix.

Does creeping thyme stay green in winter?

A Minnesota Ask Extension answer says it is "even green through the winter under the snow."
