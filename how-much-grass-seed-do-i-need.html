How Much Grass Seed Do I Need?

By Jon P. — founder of LawnSeedPicker and home-yard DIY enthusiast · Published July 24, 2026 · Updated July 24, 2026

Two numbers decide it: how many square feet you're seeding, and the seeding rate for your grass type. Multiply them and you have your answer. For a new lawn, most cool-season grasses run somewhere between 2 and 8 pounds per 1,000 square feet depending on species — tall fescue at the high end, Kentucky bluegrass at the low end — and some warm-season grasses, like bermudagrass and zoysia, need far less.

Start with your square footage

Every seeding rate you'll see is quoted per 1,000 square feet, so the area is the number you need first. For a rectangle, length times width. For an L-shaped yard, split it into rectangles and add them together. Circles are radius times radius times 3.14. Subtract the house footprint, driveway, beds, and anything else you won't be seeding — people routinely overbuy by measuring the whole lot rather than the grass.

Once you have the area, the rest is arithmetic: divide by 1,000, then multiply by your grass's rate. If you'd rather skip that step, the LawnSeedPicker calculator does it from your ZIP code and square footage and tells you both the grass to plant and the pounds to buy.

Multiply the rate by your area in thousands of square feet. A 5,000 square-foot tall fescue lawn at 6 pounds per 1,000 needs 30 pounds. Seeding by the acre instead? Multiply the per-1,000 figure by 43.

One caveat on the warm-season rows: bermudagrass, zoysia, bahiagrass and buffalograss rest on NC State Extension and USDA figures alone, because our other rate sources — UMass, Rutgers, Penn State and Cornell — are all cool-season only and offer no northern cross-check for them.

More is not better

This is the part most people get wrong, and it's worth getting right before we talk about adjustments. Seeding heavier than recommended does not produce a thicker lawn. NC State Extension states plainly that higher seeding rates produce weak, thin stands that are more susceptible to disease.

UMass Extension explains the mechanism: seeding at excessive rates produces a stand containing a high number of small, immature plants, and those are slow to develop into the mature, robust plants that tolerate heat, drought, cold and wear. Their advice is to resist the "more must be better" temptation, because overdoing it delays the lawn rather than accelerating it. Seeding too light has its own cost — an open, low-density stand that invites weeds — which is why the table above is a range to land inside rather than a floor to exceed.

Keep this straight: the rate that ends up on your soil should stay inside the table range. Everything below changes how much product you buy or where in the range you aim — not how heavily you spread.

Where you land in the range

The ranges exist because not every seed that germinates survives. UMass puts actual field germination and survival anywhere from 95% down to 50%: about 95% under ideal conditions, and roughly 70% under merely good ones. The published ranges are given specifically to allow for that variation — so if your seedbed preparation and post-planting care will be near ideal, use the lower end; if conditions are good but not ideal, use the higher end.

What pushes you toward the top of the range: poor soil (drainage, pH, compaction, salinity), an improper seedbed (too little or too much firming, rocks and debris, poor seed-to-soil contact, steep slopes), seeding at a less-than-ideal time of year, and inadequate watering or mowing after planting.

Only if conditions are exceptionally poor does UMass suggest going above the published rates, by roughly 50%. Treat that as the edge case it is. If your seedbed is bad enough to warrant it, fixing the seedbed is almost always the better investment — and seeding into a good one is cheaper than seeding into a bad one, because better conditions require less seed.

What's actually in the bag

Here is the adjustment almost every seeding-rate article skips. A bag of grass seed is not entirely grass seed. Seed labels are required to list the percentage by weight of inert matter — chaff and other material that is not seed at all — alongside the purity and germination percentages.

Coated seed is where this bites. Seed coatings are inert weight, not seed, and NC State notes that many coatings therefore require a higher seeding rate. Penn State makes the same point from the regulatory side: inert material normally cannot exceed 15% of a bag by weight, and that cap is explicitly waived when fertilizer coatings or pelleting are used. A coated bag can legally be a much larger fraction of not-seed, and you are buying that weight.

You don't have to guess at the size of the effect, because it shows up in the purity figure on the label — which is exactly what the pure live seed calculation uses.

Pure live seed, in one line

Multiply the purity percentage by the germination percentage and divide by 100. That is your PLS. To find how much seed to actually buy, divide the PLS into 100. Penn State's worked example: 85% pure seed times 72% germination, divided by 100, is 61% PLS; 100 divided by 61 is 1.6, so you'd need 1.6 pounds of that seed for every pound the recommended rate calls for.

Crucially, you only need this correction if your label falls below the standard. Penn State's published rates already assume minimum purity and germination standards — 95% purity and 80% germination for tall fescue and the fine fescues, 90% and 80% for Kentucky bluegrass, 95% and 85% for perennial ryegrass. Meet those numbers and the table rate is already the right amount to buy. Fall short of them — as coated and bargain seed often does — and you buy proportionally more to put the same living seed on the ground.

That word proportionally is doing real work. Penn State's 100 ÷ PLS formula measures against theoretically perfect seed, but their rates already build in the Table 1 minimums — so in practice the adjustment you want is the standard PLS divided by your actual PLS, not 100 divided by it. For tall fescue the standard works out to 95 × 80 ÷ 100 = 76%. Using 100 as the baseline instead would quietly raise your seeding rate rather than hold it steady, which is the opposite of the goal. That refinement is our own arithmetic, not a formula Penn State publishes.

This is also why cheap seed frequently isn't. Penn State compares two lots: one at $0.95 per pound testing 85% pure and 60% germination (51% PLS, so you need 2 pounds, costing $1.90), against one at $1.65 per pound testing 99% and 90% (89% PLS, so you need 1.1 pounds, costing $1.82). The bargain lot is the more expensive of the two per pound of viable seed. Worth a look at how to read a seed label before you buy.

One worked example, start to finish

A 5,000 square-foot lawn, seeded to tall fescue, with a well-prepared seedbed in early fall.

  1. Target rate. Tall fescue is 6–8 lb per 1,000 sq ft. Conditions are good, so aim at the low end: 6 lb per 1,000.
  2. Multiply by area. 5,000 sq ft is five lots of 1,000. 6 × 5 = 30 lb of live seed needed on the ground.
  3. Check the label. If the bag meets the standard (95% purity, 80% germination), buy 30 lb and you're done.
  4. If it falls short, correct for it. Say the bag tests 85% purity and 72% germination. PLS = 85 × 72 ÷ 100 = 61%. Measured against tall fescue's 76% standard, that's 76 ÷ 61 = 1.24. So 30 × 1.24 = about 37 lb of product to buy.

Now the part that matters: you still spread that 37 lb across the same 5,000 square feet. It delivers the same living seed to the soil that 30 lb of standard-grade seed would have. The bag got heavier to make up for weaker seed; the target did not move.

What you must not do is read the bigger bag as permission to spread heavier. Buying 37 lb and then broadcasting it as though the rate had risen to 9 or 10 pounds per 1,000 square feet is precisely the over-seeding that produces a thin, disease-prone stand.

Overseeding uses less seed than a new lawn

If you're thickening an existing lawn rather than starting from bare soil, the rates above are too high. NC State's guidance is to use new-establishment rates only when seeding bare ground; when seeding into existing vegetation, a rate 25–50% lower is appropriate. On a 6–8 pound tall fescue rate, that's roughly 3 to 6 pounds per 1,000 square feet instead. Our full guide to overseeding in fall vs. spring covers the timing and the method.

Rates travel; planting dates don't

Seeding rates hold up reasonably well across the country — a pound of tall fescue seed covers the same ground in Georgia as in Michigan. Planting dates do not. NC State's rates come paired with dates calibrated to North Carolina's mountains, piedmont and coastal plain, and those dates shouldn't be lifted north.

For northern lawns, late summer is the preferred window: UMass points to it because warm soils speed germination and the two to three months of favorable growth before winter arrive during a period of reduced weed competition. Rutgers agrees that the best time to seed cool-season grasses is late summer or early fall, adding that spring seeding can work but may need a preemergence herbicide to hold back crabgrass. Timing by region is covered in when to plant grass seed.

Why the bag says something different

Three reasons a number on a seed bag or another website won't match the table above.

Mixture rates are not species rates. The table gives rates for single-species stands. A blend is seeded at a rate for the blend as a whole, not the sum of its parts. Penn State seeds a mix of 40–60% Kentucky bluegrass, 30–40% fine fescue and 10–20% perennial ryegrass at 3–4 pounds per 1,000 square feet total — well under what tall fescue alone would need. Likewise, NC State lists fine fescue at 1.5 pounds as a component of a mix, not as a stand on its own. Comparing a mixture number to a single-species number is the most common way people talk themselves into the wrong amount.

Not every rate table is written for lawns. Cornell's seeding-rate guidance sits inside its sports field management program, where the governing question is how soon a field can take play — a different problem from a home lawn. Professional turf tables differ again.

And sometimes sources simply disagree. UMass's turf program lists perennial ryegrass at 7–9 pounds per 1,000 square feet, well above the 4–6 in the table. For what it's worth, our own read is that UMass rates ryegrass like tall fescue because the two have almost identical seed counts per pound — but that's our inference from comparing the tables, not a conclusion any extension source states. We've stayed with the more widely supported 4–6 for lawns.

Frequently asked questions

How much grass seed do I need for 1,000 square feet?

It depends entirely on the grass. For a new lawn: about 2 to 3 pounds for Kentucky bluegrass, 4 to 6 for perennial ryegrass, 6 to 8 for tall fescue, and 4 to 6 for fine fescues. Warm-season grasses need less — 1 to 2 pounds for seeded bermudagrass or zoysia, about 5 for bahiagrass, and 3 to 5 for buffalograss.

How many pounds of grass seed per acre?

Take the per-1,000-square-foot rate and multiply by 43. Tall fescue at 6 to 8 pounds per 1,000 works out to roughly 258 to 344 pounds per acre; Kentucky bluegrass at 2 to 3 pounds comes to about 86 to 129.

Does coated grass seed need a higher rate?

You need to buy more of it, yes. The coating is inert weight rather than seed, so a coated bag contains less actual seed per pound — and the normal 15% cap on inert material is waived for coated and pelleted products. The effect shows up in the purity percentage on the label, so running the pure live seed calculation accounts for it. What doesn't change is how heavily you spread: the goal is still the same amount of living seed reaching the soil.

What happens if I use too much grass seed?

You get a worse lawn, not a better one. Over-seeding produces a dense stand of small, immature plants that compete with each other, develop slowly, and tolerate heat, drought and wear poorly — and the resulting thin turf is more susceptible to disease. Seeding too lightly is also a mistake, leaving an open stand that weeds move into, which is why the recommended figures are ranges to aim inside.

How much seed do I need for overseeding rather than a new lawn?

Roughly 25 to 50% less than a new-lawn rate, because you're supplementing existing grass instead of covering bare soil. For tall fescue that means about 3 to 6 pounds per 1,000 square feet rather than 6 to 8.
